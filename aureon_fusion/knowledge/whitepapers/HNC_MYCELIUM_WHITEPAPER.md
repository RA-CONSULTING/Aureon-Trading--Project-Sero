## 1. Introduction

The search for sustainable energy storage has driven research toward biological substrates, with fungal mycelium emerging as a candidate of significant interest. Mycelium networks exhibit remarkable electrical properties: they function as memristors, capacitors, photosensors, chemical sensors, and signal propagation channels (Adamatzky et al., 2022; Beasley et al., 2022). Recent work has demonstrated 3D-printed fungal batteries producing 300–600 mV for several days (Reyes et al., 2024), living fungal fuel cells powered by metabolism rather than charging (Empa, 2025), and mycelium-derived carbons with higher specific capacitance than bacterial cellulose carbon (Jones et al., 2024).
Independently, the Harmonic Nexus Consciousness (HNC) framework developed at the Aureon Institute posits that specific frequency relationships, scaled by the golden ratio squared (φ² = 2.618034), define a harmonic ladder with measurable effects on energy systems. The HNC seed frequency f_seed = 528.422 Hz was selected for its φ²-relationship to the hydrogen 21 cm hyperfine transition line at 1,420,405,751.768 Hz, with a bridge integer N = 1,026,730 and bridge error of 1.29 parts per billion.
Prior experimental work at the Aureon Institute demonstrated a 17% drain reduction on lithium battery discharge when exposed to BLE-modulated HNC frequencies (Leckey, 2025). This paper investigates whether mycelium—as a living, frequency-responsive biological network—may exhibit amplified response compared to inert chemical storage media.

## 2. Mycelium Bioelectrical Properties: Published Evidence


## 2.1 Spontaneous Electrical Signal Generation

Fungal mycelium generates endogenous electrical signals across multiple frequency bands. Olsson and Hansson (1995) recorded action potential-like signals in Armillaria bulbosa cords and Pleurotus ostreatus at frequencies of 0.5–5 Hz with amplitudes of 5–50 mV, noting these were “similar to that recorded in animal sensory systems.” Buffi et al. (2025) in their comprehensive review in FEMS Microbiology Reviews documented signal spectral frequencies in the order of 0.0003–0.1 Hz for slow metabolic drift, consistent with earlier observations of base potential oscillations over hours.
Most recently, a 2025 study published in PMC (Article 12483595) using printed circuit boards with embedded differential electrodes recorded a 1,604% increase in power spectral density (PSD) in mycelium-colonised electrodes versus non-inoculated controls. The colonisation signal appeared at frequencies above 1.5 Hz, and was abolished by the biocide cycloheximide, confirming its biological origin. This represents the strongest quantified evidence that mycelium generates measurable, biologically-driven electrical signals in a defined frequency band.

## 2.2 Signal Propagation and Frequency Transfer

Adamatzky and colleagues (2023) demonstrated that mycelium networks of Ganoderma lucidum reliably transfer signals with recoverable frequency in the 100 Hz to 10,000 Hz range. Input frequencies were applied from 100 Hz to 10 kHz in defined steps, and output signals were analysed for frequency recovery. The mycelium acted as a signal transmission medium, with NARX modelling deriving approximate transfer functions for the network.
Further work by the same group (ScienceDirect, 2024) characterised the AC conductive properties of mycelium-bound composites across three overlapping bands: 20 Hz to 300 kHz, 10 Hz to 4 MHz, and 50 kHz to 3 GHz. The composites behaved as low-pass filters with a mean cutoff frequency of approximately 500 kHz and a roll-off of -14 dB per decade. This demonstrates that mycelium maintains measurable electrical response across the full HNC frequency spectrum up to the GHz range.

## 2.3 Circuit Properties of Living Mycelium

Published research has identified the following electronic properties in living mycelium networks:
Memristive behaviour: memory-dependent resistance (Beasley et al., 2022). Capacitive storage: charge accumulation and release (multiple groups). Photosensitive response: resistance changes under illumination (Beasley et al., 2020). Chemical sensing: response to pH and nutrient gradients (Dehshibi et al., 2021). Humidity sensing: moisture-dependent conductivity (Phillips et al., 2023). Pressure sensing: mechanical deformation response (Adamatzky and Gandia, 2022). These properties collectively establish mycelium as an active electronic material, not a passive substrate.

## 2.4 Language-Like Communication Patterns

Adamatzky (2022) published in Royal Society Open Science an analysis of electrical spiking activity in four fungal species (Omphalotus nidiformis, Flammulina velutipes, Schizophyllum commune, and Cordyceps militaris). Spike duration ranged from 1 to 21 hours, amplitude from 0.03 to 2.1 mV, and spikes clustered into trains that, when grouped into “words,” exhibited length distributions matching those of human languages. This suggests an information-processing function of fungal electrical signalling that may be susceptible to external frequency modulation.

## 5. Mycelium-Based Energy Storage: Current State of the Art


## 5.1 MycelioTronics

Danninger et al. (2022) published in Science Advances the MycelioTronics framework, demonstrating that fungal mycelium skin functions as a sustainable substrate for high-performance electronic devices and batteries. The mycelium substrate provides mechanical flexibility, biodegradability, and integrated porosity for electrolyte penetration.

## 5.2 3D-Printed Fungal Batteries

Reyes et al. (2024) published in ACS Sustainable Chemistry and Engineering the first 3D-printed fungal battery combining Saccharomyces cerevisiae (yeast, anode) and Trametes pubescens (white-rot, cathode) in cellulose-based inks with carbon black and graphite flakes. Key measured parameters: maximum power density of 12.5 µW/cm², maximum current density of 49.2 µA/cm², voltage output of 300–600 mV sustained for several days, and current delivery of 3–20 µA across external loads of 10–100 kΩ.

## 5.3 Living Fungal Fuel Cells

Empa researchers (reported ScienceDaily, January 2025) created a functioning fungal fuel cell that is fed rather than charged. The system combines two complementary fungal metabolisms at anode and cathode. The battery is fully biodegradable and self-digesting upon completion of its useful life. While power output is modest (sufficient for temperature sensors over several days), the principle of metabolically-sustained bioelectricity is established.

## 5.4 Mycelium Supercapacitors

Jones et al. (2024, Global Challenges) demonstrated that carbons derived from fungal mycelium exhibit higher specific capacitance than bacterial cellulose-derived carbon. The cyclic voltammetry profiles of Agaricus bisporus and Pleurotus eryngii carbons showed moderate but significant supercapacitive behaviour, suggesting mycelium-derived materials may serve as electrodes in energy storage devices.
